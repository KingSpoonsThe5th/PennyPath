Budgeting

Budgeting helps balance your income and expenses so you can save money.

The 50/30/20 rule

50%
30%
20%

50% Needs

Rent or mortgage, utilities, groceries, health insurance, and car payments.

30% Wants

Dining out, streaming services, hobbies, and gym memberships.

20% Savings & Debt

Emergency fund contributions, retirement accounts, and extra debt payments.

Tips

  • Automatically transfer a set savings or investment amount out of your paycheck the moment it arrives, before you pay bills.
  • Divide cash for variable spending like groceries or entertainment; when the money in that category runs out, spending in that area stops for the month.
  • Know your true net income.
  • Set up direct deposits or recurring auto transfers for bills and savings so you don’t have to rely on willpower.
  • When your income increases, try to boost your savings rate instead of automatically expanding your daily spending.

See it in dollars

Say your take-home pay is $3,000 a month:

$1,500

Needs

Rent, utilities, groceries, insurance, car.

$900

Wants

Dining out, streaming, hobbies, gym.

$600

Savings & debt

Emergency fund, retirement, extra debt payments.

Other ways to budget

Zero-based

Give every dollar a job until income minus planned spending equals zero.

Pay yourself first

Save before you spend, then live on what’s left.

Track, then adjust

Review your spending monthly. A budget is a living plan, not a one-time setup.

02

Saving & Emergency Funds

Saving is the process of keeping some of the money you receive instead of spending it immediately.

!

Emergency savings

For unexpected, urgent situations.

★

Goal-based savings

Saving for something you want or need in the future.

∞

Long-term savings

Money you put away for years down the road, like when you want to buy a house.

Building your emergency fund

  • A common target is 3 to 6 months of essential expenses. Start with a smaller goal, like one month, and build up.
  • Keep it somewhere safe and easy to reach, like a savings account, not tied up in investments that can drop in value.
  • A high-yield savings account pays more interest than a standard one, so your cushion grows while it sits.
  • Keep it separate from your checking account so you’re not tempted to spend it.
  • For predictable big costs like car repairs, holidays, or travel, set aside a little each month in a labeled “sinking fund.”

Credit Scores

Lenders, landlords, and other companies use your score to measure your financial risk and decide whether to approve you for loans, credit cards, or housing.

Score ranges

Exceptional800 – 850
Very Good740 – 799
Good670 – 739
Fair580 – 669
Poor300 – 579

How to increase your credit score

  • Pay every bill on time.
  • Lower your credit utilization ratio (under 30%, ideally under 10%).
  • Keep older accounts open.
  • Check your credit reports for errors.
  • Limit new applications.

What makes up your score

Based on the widely used FICO model:

A variety of account types, like cards and loans.

Good to know

  • A hard inquiry happens when you apply for credit and can dip your score slightly. Checking your own score is a soft inquiry and doesn’t hurt it.
  • You can get free copies of your credit reports from the three major bureaus at AnnualCreditReport.com.
  • Utilization example: a $1,000 limit with a $300 balance is 30% utilization.

Loans & Debt Management

A loan is an agreement in which a lender gives you money that you promise to repay under specific terms.

The 4 basic parts of a loan

1

Principal

The original amount you borrow.

2

Interest

The cost of borrowing money; the lender charges interest according to the loan agreement.

3

Term

How long you have to repay the loan.

4

Repayment schedule

Determines how much is due and when. Most loans use fixed monthly payments.

Terms worth knowing

APR

The yearly cost of a loan, including interest and some fees. Use it to compare offers.

Fixed vs. variable

A fixed rate stays the same. A variable rate can rise or fall over time.

Secured vs. unsecured

Secured loans are backed by collateral, like a car or home. Unsecured loans aren’t.

Two ways to pay off debt

Avalanche

Pay extra toward the highest interest rate first. This usually saves the most money.

Snowball

Pay off the smallest balance first. The quick wins can keep you motivated.

Either way, pay at least the minimum on everything and send the extra to one target.

Taxes & Paychecks

Understand where your money goes before it ever hits your account.

Reading your paycheck

Gross pay

What you earn before anything is taken out.

Deductions

Federal and state income tax, Social Security and Medicare (FICA), plus things like health insurance premiums and retirement contributions.

Net pay

Your take-home pay, the number to build your budget around.

Key tax forms

W-4

Withholding

You fill it out for your employer to tell them how much tax to take from each paycheck.

W-2

Employee summary

Sent by your employer each year, showing what you earned and what was withheld.

1099

Freelance income

Taxes usually aren’t withheld, so you may need to make estimated payments during the year.

Good to know

  • Tax brackets are marginal: only the income inside each bracket is taxed at that bracket’s rate, not all of it.
  • A deduction lowers the income you’re taxed on. A credit lowers your tax bill dollar for dollar.
  • A big refund isn’t a bonus. It usually means too much was withheld from your paychecks all year.
  • Contributions to a traditional 401(k) come out before income tax, which lowers your taxable income.
  • If your employer offers a retirement match, contribute enough to get all of it. It’s free money.
  • Check your pay stub regularly and keep tax records, like W-2s and receipts, in one place.

Investing & Retirement

Investing means buying assets that you expect to grow in value or generate income, or both.

Putting $1,000 into a diversified stock fund is investing. Putting $1,000 in a savings account that earns interest is just saving.

Stocks

Partial ownership in a company.

Bonds

A loan to a government, company, or municipality. Typically promises interest and repayment of principal.

Index funds & ETFs

An index fund holds investments and tracks a particular market index, while ETFs trade on exchanges like stocks.

Real estate

Can bring in rental income or appreciation, but involves maintenance, taxes, vacancies, and lots of upfront costs.

Commodities

Gold or silver and similar assets can be volatile and hard to value.

Crypto Super risky

A speculative investment.

Core ideas

  • Compounding: your earnings start earning too, so time is your biggest advantage. Starting early matters.
  • Diversification: spreading money across many investments so one bad one doesn’t sink you.
  • Risk and return: higher possible returns generally come with bigger possible losses.
  • Time horizon: money you need soon shouldn’t be in volatile investments.

Retirement accounts

401(k)

Offered through an employer, often with a company match.

Traditional IRA

You open it yourself. Contributions may be tax-deductible, and you pay tax when you withdraw.

Roth IRA

You contribute money you’ve already paid tax on, and qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free.

07

Insurance

Protect yourself without overpaying.

  • Avoid choosing the cheapest state-minimum car insurance just to lower your monthly insurance bill. State minimums rarely cover the total cost of a serious accident.
  • Insure your home for rebuilding cost, not market value.
  • Purchase your auto and homeowners or renters insurance from the same carrier to unlock multi-policy discounts.
  • Understand your deductibles and out-of-pocket costs.

Common types

Health

Covers medical care costs.

Auto

Covers damage and liability from accidents.

Home or renters

Covers your belongings and, for homeowners, the structure.

Life

Pays your loved ones if you die. Matters most if others depend on your income.

Disability

Replaces part of your income if you can’t work.

Premium vs. deductible

  • The premium is what you pay regularly to keep the policy.
  • The deductible is what you pay yourself on a claim before insurance kicks in. A higher deductible usually means a lower premium, so make sure you could afford it.

Fraud & Scam Prevention

Know the red flags before they know you.

Red flags

  • Manufactured urgency: They insist you must act immediately or face terrible consequences, giving you no time to think or consult someone else.
  • They insist on payment via cryptocurrency, gift cards, wire transfers (Western Union/MoneyGram), or untraceable peer-to-peer apps.
  • Legitimate government agencies and banks will never call, text, or email asking for your PIN, passwords, full Social Security number, or verification codes.
  • Too good to be true: Investments promising guaranteed high returns or job postings offering big pay for zero effort are almost always traps.

What to do

Stop & verify

Verify independently.

Guard your codes

Never share verification codes.

Lock it down

Enable 2-step verification.

Talk first

Talk to someone first.

Report it

Report the scam.

Common scams

Phishing

Fake emails or texts with links that steal your logins. Go to the real site yourself instead of clicking.

Impersonation

Someone pretends to be your bank, the IRS, or a relative in trouble.

Romance scams

An online “partner” you’ve never met asks for money.

Tech support

A pop-up or call claims your device is infected and demands payment or remote access.

Fake checks

You’re sent a check, told to deposit it and send some money back. The check bounces later.

If you’ve been scammed

  • Contact your bank or card issuer right away and ask them to stop or reverse the payment.
  • Change passwords on affected accounts and turn on 2-step verification.
  • Consider placing a freeze on your credit reports if personal info was exposed.
  • Report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.
